# Presenting Ha Koo Soong Wai to the judges

A script, a demo order, and prepared answers to the questions you should expect.

> **Read this first.** Everything in the app is illustrative demo data — the 318
> mentors, the 1,284 hours, the ฿ saving. They are there to make the model legible,
> not to be cited. If a judge asks "where did that number come from", the honest
> answer is *"that is illustrative — we'd size it from district data in a real
> pilot"*, and that answer scores better than a number you can't defend. The
> **What's real and what's mocked up?** section below tells you which claims you
> can stand behind and which you can't.

---

## The one sentence

> **You cannot hire enough people to care for an ageing population. But you can
> train the ageing population to care for itself.**

If you only get one sentence, that is the one.

## The framing that wins this

Thailand already has roughly **a million village health volunteers (อสม.)** — one
of the most successful community health programmes in the world, and something a
Thai judge is proud of. *(Verify the current figure with the Ministry of Public
Health before quoting it — it is widely published, but quote it accurately.)*

So you are not asking anyone to believe in a fantasy. You are saying:

> "Thailand already proved that trained community members can carry primary care.
> We are extending that model to the one group that is growing while every other
> workforce shrinks — older people themselves — and giving it a digital spine."

That reframes you from *"a nice student idea"* to *"the next version of
infrastructure Thailand already runs."* Lead with it.

## The app

**Ha Koo Soong Wai** (หาคู่สูงวัย) is one platform with two sides. Present them in
this order and the second side lands much harder.

### Side one — young volunteers

*"Youths for a kinder tomorrow."*

Matches a verified young volunteer with an elderly neighbour who has asked for
help — companionship, a grocery trip, a walk in the park. Shows a match
percentage, distance, what the person needs, and books a one-time visit at
**฿350 per visit**.

**What it proves:** the matching model works, and it produces real contact with
people who would otherwise have none. Match, verify, schedule, visit. It is the
proof that this kind of connection can be manufactured on demand.

*Side one is a separate, already-built app — this prototype is side two. Present
it briefly as the foundation, then spend your time on side two.*

**Where it stops:** it is a **market**. Every visit is paid. Every visit needs a
young person with free time and a reason to spend it on a stranger. Add up what
it costs to give one isolated person a visit a week and the arithmetic stops
working at national scale — and the young volunteers are the same people who will
be working full-time in 2050.

### Side two — the senior mentor network (this prototype)

*"Seniors for a stronger community."*

The same matching insight, aimed one level up: **the volunteers are the elderly.**

A healthy 68-year-old signs up. The **community health centre trains her** —
five clinician-written modules, ending in a quiz and a certificate signed by a
named nurse. Now a *certified peer health mentor*, she takes a structured
check-in to the neighbours she supports: blood pressure, mood, appetite,
medication, dizziness on standing, falls, home hazards.

The app applies **the clinic's own thresholds** to what she records — she never
judges severity, the rules do. When something is out of range she sends it to the
nurse, who triages it from her desk in minutes.

**The shift in one line:**

> **Side one is a market — you pay someone to care. Side two is a circle — the
> people who need care become the people who give it.**

That is what "2050" actually means here: not more professionals, but a different
answer to *who* a health system is made of.

## The demo — do it in this order

Roughly two minutes. Narrate each step with the line under it.

**1. Academy → *Checking blood pressure and pulse* → read the lessons → quiz → certify.**
> "This is the part that makes it safe. She isn't a well-meaning amateur — she is
> trained by a named clinician and certified against the health centre's standard.
> Notice the course tells her the clinic's exact thresholds, and tells her
> plainly what she is *not* authorised to do."

*Point at the certificate screen: it states in writing that she cannot diagnose,
cannot advise on medication, and cannot act in an emergency beyond calling for help.*

**2. Circle → Mr. Prasert → Log a check-in. Push systolic to ~170. Report dizziness on standing and a fall. Save.**
> "She's not filling in a form. She's taking a structured observation a clinic
> would take six months to make — because he'd never book an appointment for a
> dizzy spell."

**3. The red verdict appears. → Send this to Nurse Anong.**
> "The app applied the clinic's rules. She didn't have to decide whether 170 was
> serious — that isn't her job, and that's exactly what makes it safe to delegate."

**4. Switch to the health professional view. Open the flag. Triage it.**
> "This is the whole argument on one screen. A neighbour noticed something, and a
> nurse acted on it in minutes without leaving her desk — with the mentor's
> verbatim note, the readings, and the history in front of her."

**5. Switch back → Mr. Prasert. Her decision is on his record.**
> "And the loop closes. Notice the rail on the left — the counters move as we go,
> because the model only works if the system-level number moves too."

Then, to finish: **open Impact** and point at **cost per check-in: ฿0**.
> "Six thousand eight hundred check-ins. The equivalent in paid home visits would
> be over three million baht. That is what changes when the workforce is
> volunteers who gain health from the work itself."

## The question you must have an answer for

**"Why would a healthy 68-year-old do this for free?"**

This is the question the whole idea turns on. Do not answer it with
"volunteering is nice". Answer it with three concrete things:

1. **For a mentor, volunteering *is* the treatment.** Older adults who volunteer
   regularly show lower mortality, less depression and slower cognitive decline
   than those who don't. She is not spending her health on someone else — she is
   maintaining her own. *(This is well established in the gerontology literature;
   if a judge pushes, say you'd cite specific cohort studies in a fuller paper.)*

2. **The credential is real.** Her certificate is issued by the community health
   centre and signed by a named professional. That is status, purpose and a role
   in her community — the three things retirement takes away at once. Side one pays
   ฿350. This pays in standing, and standing doesn't run out.

3. **Reciprocity, not charity.** Today's mentor is tomorrow's recipient. Everyone
   in the network is both. That is why it survives — a market stops when the money
   stops; a circle doesn't.

## Other questions to expect

**"Isn't this just exploiting unpaid elderly labour?"**
> The opposite — it targets the people retirement leaves without purpose, and
> gives them a credentialed role. It is unpaid the way being a grandparent is
> unpaid. And it is capped deliberately: four to six neighbours each, so it never
> becomes a job.

**"What if a mentor misses something serious, or gets it wrong?"**
> The design assumes she will. She never diagnoses and never touches medication —
> the curriculum says so and the certificate says so in writing. The severity call
> is made by the clinic's thresholds in code, not by her judgement. Anything that
> trips a threshold goes to a nurse the same day. The mentor is a sensor, not a
> clinician. That is a deliberate, narrow, safe role.

**"Is the elderly person being experimented on?"**
> No. Every mentor is certified against the health centre's standard, and every
> clinical decision stays with the nurse. The mentor widens the nurse's reach; she
> never replaces her authority.

**"Where's the money / who pays?"**
> Compared with any alternative, it is close to free. The marginal cost per
> contact is zero because the workforce is unpaid and already lives next door.
> Public health systems already fund อสม. training and community health centres —
> this adds a digital layer and a certification path onto structures that exist.

**"Why does this need an app at all? Can't they just visit?"**
> Three reasons, and this is the interesting part. **Consistency** — the same
> check-in, every time, so the numbers are comparable. **Thresholds** — the app
> holds the clinic's rules, so the safety decision isn't made by an untrained
> person. **The record** — one blood pressure says nothing; twelve over three
> months is a trend, and a trend is what a doctor can act on. A paper system gives
> you none of the three.

**"What's real and what's mocked up?"**
> Answer this straight. **Real:** the model, the training content and thresholds
> (drawn from standard practice), the escalation logic, and every screen — it all
> works, you can click it. **Illustrative:** the district numbers and the ฿ saving.
> **Not built:** identity verification, integration with real health records, and
> a pilot to measure outcomes. Say so before they find it.

## If you have thirty seconds and nothing else

> "Thailand already proved that trained community volunteers can carry primary
> care. Ha Koo Soong Wai starts by paying young people to visit isolated seniors
> — and it works, but it's a market, and markets don't scale to a country where a
> third of the population will be over sixty. So we changed who the volunteer is.
> Healthy seniors get trained and certified by the health centre, then check on
> their neighbours with a structured tool that applies the clinic's own rules.
> The elderly stop being the cost of the health system and become part of its
> workforce. In the demo you can watch a neighbour catch something, send it to a
> nurse, and get an answer — in under two minutes."

## Two things worth adding before you present

1. **The อสม. comparison, as a slide.** One line — "Thailand's village health
   volunteers, extended to the ageing population" — does more for your credibility
   than any feature list.
2. **A real number from a real source.** Pick one figure on Thailand's ageing
   (share of population over 60 by 2050) from the National Statistical Office or
   the UN, and put it on your first slide. One sourced number beats ten invented ones.
